This chapter establishes the technical foundations on which the thesis rests. It
opens with the industrial setting and its threat landscape, surveys the families
of artificial-intelligence models available for security learning, and then
narrows to the learning-based mechanisms that turn behavioural evidence into an
enforceable trust decision. It next introduces the distributed ledgers that make
such decisions tamper-evident, the hybrid ledger architectures that reconcile
operational speed with external verifiability, and the post-quantum cryptography
on which long-lived device identity must now rely. The chapter closes by drawing
these threads into the case for trust-partitioned security.

\section{Industrial IoT Security Landscape}
\label{sec:bg-landscape}

This section establishes the terrain on which the work is built. It defines the
Industrial Internet of Things and its mission-critical role, traces the evolution
that has led from isolated industrial control systems to partitioned,
quantum-aware trust architectures, characterises the devices and the threat
landscape that deployment at scale invites, and reviews the regulatory and
standards context within which any deployable system must sit.

\subsection{From Industrial Control Systems to Trust-Partitioned IIoT}
\label{subsec:bg-evolution}

Industrial computing has passed through several distinct eras, and the security
problem addressed here is a product of that progression. Early industrial
control systems were isolated: programmable logic controllers and supervisory
systems operated on air-gapped networks, and security rested largely on physical
separation rather than on cryptography~\cite{hussain2025survey}. The Industrial
Internet of Things dissolved that
separation~\cite{enemosah2024scada, kebande2024industrial}. By interconnecting
sensors, controllers, actuators, and gateways over shared networks, IIoT
delivered real-time monitoring and adaptive control, but it also exposed
formerly isolated equipment to remote
adversaries~\cite{kayan2022cybersecurity, zhukabayeva2025cybersecurity}.
Subsequent phases layered artificial intelligence onto this fabric for
behavioural anomaly detection~\cite{pinto2023survey, nuaimi2023intelligent},
and then introduced blockchain to provide tamper-evident provenance for the data
and decisions that AI produced~\cite{amadi2026security, khan2022internet,
huo2022comprehensive}. The present phase, the one STRATA occupies, adds two
further requirements: that trust be partitioned across tiered ledgers so that
heavyweight security work does not fall on the weakest devices, and that the
cryptography underpinning identity and key exchange be resilient against an
emerging quantum adversary. Fig.~\ref{fig:iiot-evolution} traces this
progression and situates the present contribution at its endpoint.

\subsection{Scale, Mission-Critical Role, and Constrained Devices}
\label{subsec:bg-scale}

The IIoT is now infrastructure at a scale that makes its security a matter of
public consequence rather than private inconvenience. The growth of connected
devices summarised in Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape} is matched by the growth of the
data they produce: IDC's Data Age 2025 study forecast that the global
datasphere, the total volume of data created, captured, and replicated worldwide
each year, would expand from 45~zettabytes in 2019 to 175~zettabytes by
2025~\cite{idc_dataage2025}. A growing share of this base is embedded in
manufacturing, energy, water, and transportation systems, where the data
exchanged does not merely inform but directly actuates physical processes. In
such settings a corrupted reading, a forged command, or a delayed validation is
not a data-quality issue but a safety and continuity issue~\cite{yu2023survey},
and this is what distinguishes industrial security from its enterprise
counterpart~\cite{lehto2022cyber}.

The devices that carry this burden are, for the most part, constrained. The
Internet Engineering Task Force classifies constrained nodes by the memory
available to them, and the classes provide a common vocabulary for the budgets
referred to throughout this thesis~\cite{rfc7228}. Table~\ref{tab:device-classes}
summarises the classes and their implications for security. Sensors, actuators,
and field controllers typically fall into Classes~0 to~2, where a full protocol
stack, a deep detector, and post-quantum cryptography cannot all be
accommodated at once, while gateways and edge servers above these classes can
host aggregation, validation, and ledger participation. The distance between the
two ends of this range is the reason security work must be placed deliberately
rather than applied uniformly.

\subsection{The Threat Landscape}
\label{subsec:bg-threats}

The consequence of pervasive connectivity is a substantially enlarged attack
surface. Operational-technology environments, once peripheral to attacker
interest, have become primary targets. Ransomware activity against industrial
organisations rose by 87\% year over year, the number of ransomware groups
directing effort at such organisations grew by 60\% to reach 80, and
manufacturing alone accounted for more than half of observed victims, with
roughly a quarter of incidents forcing a full shutdown of the affected
site~\cite{dragos2025}. Beyond their headline magnitude, these figures matter
because industrial incidents convert directly into physical disruption, which
raises the stakes of every security decision at the device
level~\cite{yu2023survey}.

The specific forms these attacks take on industrial networks can be organised by
their traffic-level behaviour~\cite{aslam2024scrutinizing, sasi2024comprehensive}.
Denial-of-service and distributed denial-of-service attacks manifest as
high-rate flooding that saturates controllers and gateways; reconnaissance
appears as systematic probing of control-protocol ports; credential attacks
show as repeated authentication failures against brokers and controller
interfaces; and infiltration and botnet activity appear as anomalous payload
entropy and periodic beaconing~\cite{gelgi2024systematic}.

manifestations, following the characterisation used in the behavioural detection
work of Chapter~\ref{Chapter_4}, and Fig.~\ref{fig:iiot-stack} locates the
dominant threats at each tier of a deployment.

\subsection{Regulatory and Standards Context}
\label{subsec:bg-regulatory}

Any deployable industrial security system sits within a regulatory and
standards environment that is itself evolving. The strategic value of
industrial data sharing is increasingly recognised in policy, with the EU Data
Act of 2023 establishing harmonised rules on fair access to and use of the data
generated by connected products~\cite{eu2023dataact}. On the cryptographic side
the shift has been sharper still. The finalisation of the first post-quantum
standards by the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology in
August 2024 turned the quantum threat from a research concern into a compliance
one~\cite{fips203, fips204, fips205}, so that migration toward quantum-resilient
primitives is now an anticipated requirement rather than an optional
enhancement~\cite{wang2025review}. The technical basis of that migration, and
the difficulty it poses for constrained hardware, is developed in
Section~\ref{sec:bg-pqc}.

\section{AI Models and Cognitive Engines for IIoT Security}
\label{sec:ai-models}

Protecting constrained, mission-critical IIoT against the threat landscape of
Section~\ref{sec:bg-landscape} begins with the choice of model that observes it.
An artificial intelligence model in this setting is a function learned from data
that maps device and network observations to a security decision, and the
cognitive engine is that trained model together with the calibration and scoring
logic that turns its raw output into a usable, thresholded verdict. The model
family is decisive, because each family carries a distinct inductive bias, data
appetite, and transparency profile that fixes both what the engine can detect
and how far its decision can be trusted and audited. A margin-based classifier,
a convolutional feature extractor, and a graph model differ not merely in
accuracy but in whether their outputs are interpretable, whether their
probabilities can be calibrated, and whether their execution can be reproduced
for audit. Those differences weigh most heavily under the resource and trust
budget of an industrial device, where a model that cannot run within the device
envelope, or whose decision cannot be reconstructed, is unsuitable however
strong its benchmark accuracy.

\subsection{Model Families and the Security-Learning Tasks}
\label{subsec:model-taxonomy}

Security learning in IIoT reduces to a small set of canonical tasks: binary
anomaly detection, which separates normal from abnormal behaviour; multi-class
attack classification, which assigns an observation to a specific attack
category; and continuous behavioural trust scoring, which maps a device or flow
to a graded reliability value. Sequential and topology-aware variants extend
these tasks to telemetry that carries temporal or relational structure.
Comprehensive surveys of machine-learning and deep-learning intrusion detection
organise the field along these lines, cataloguing the algorithms, datasets, and
attack categories that recur across
deployments~\cite{ali2024unveiling, kikissagbe2024machine}. A consistent finding
across them is that each task admits treatment by more than one model family, so
family selection is a design decision rather than a fixed consequence of the
task.

Two axes organise the family space. The first is representational depth:
shallow, or classical, models operate on engineered features, whereas deep
models learn representations directly from raw or lightly processed inputs. The
second is the structure of the input: tabular flow statistics suit
feature-based classifiers, while raw packet, byte, or event sequences reward
models with spatial or temporal priors. Recent reviews of machine-learning
intrusion detection for the Internet of Things adopt a comparable organisation,
grouping methods by learning paradigm and by the representation each
consumes~\cite{manivannan2024recent}. Fig.~\ref{fig:ai-taxonomy} maps the
families reviewed here onto the tasks they serve, and
Table~\ref{tab:ai-family-comparison} compares them across the dimensions that
matter for industrial deployment.

The IIoT setting imposes a third axis that general machine-learning benchmarks
omit: the resource and trust budget. A model that cannot execute within a
Class~1 device budget (Table~\ref{tab:device-classes}), or whose outputs cannot
be calibrated and audited, is unsuitable for mission-critical use, however high
its benchmark accuracy. Comparative evaluations of lightweight classifiers
across IoT and industrial IoT datasets show that detection quality and resource
cost trade against one another, so the technique that ranks first on accuracy
is rarely the one that fits the device~\cite{ismail2025intrusion}, and
edge-oriented designs recover deployability only through explicit optimisation
such as feature reduction and model quantisation~\cite{tiwari2024lightweight}.
Surveys of security for resource-constrained devices treat this budget as a
primary design constraint rather than a secondary
concern~\cite{altaibek2025survey}. This axis reweights the comparison throughout
the section, favouring families whose cost and transparency remain within reach
of the device rather than those that maximise raw discriminative power alone.

\subsection{Classical Machine Learning}
\label{subsec:classical-ml}

Support vector machines separate classes by the maximum-margin hyperplane in a
feature space that a kernel makes implicitly nonlinear. The margin formulation
performs well in high-dimensional feature spaces and captures nonlinear attack
patterns through the kernel, which has made it a recurring choice for intrusion
detection in the Internet of Things~\cite{sama2024cutting}. Fine-tuned linear and
feature-selected variants deliver accurate detection at a small model size well
matched to constrained gateways~\cite{azimjonov2024designing}, and hybrid
rule-augmented designs extend the approach to heterogeneous IoT threat
data~\cite{ashraf2024hybrid}. The principal limitation is scalability: kernel
training scales quadratically in the number of samples, so large corpora require
dimensionality reduction or optimisation of the support-vector search to remain
tractable~\cite{raghunath2025pca}. Because the decision boundary depends only on
a compact set of support vectors, the trained model itself remains small, which
is an advantage on resource-limited hardware.

Tree ensembles occupy the opposite end of the classical spectrum. Random forests
aggregate many decorrelated decision trees by bagging, while gradient boosting
builds trees sequentially to correct the residual error of the ensemble so far.
Both handle heterogeneous tabular flow features with minimal preprocessing and
deliver strong performance out of the box, and lightweight tree-based detectors
are for this reason widely deployed on resource-constrained IoT nodes where
preprocessing and compute headroom are
scarce~\cite{mishra2025lirad, pandey2025lightweight}. Their split structure also
yields native feature-importance measures, so an analyst can trace which
features drove a decision.

Three properties shared across the classical family bear directly on trust.
Classical models are data-efficient, deterministic under fixed seeds, and
interpretable, since tree paths and linear or margin weights expose the basis of
a decision~\cite{abdelaziz2025enhancing}. Determinism in particular makes their
outputs reproducible and therefore auditable, a property that
Section~\ref{subsec:transparency-trust} develops as a trust requirement. The
countervailing limitation is that classical models depend on manual feature
engineering and do not natively represent the spatial or temporal structure
present in raw traffic, which motivates the deep architectures reviewed next.

\subsection{Deep Learning Architectures}
\label{subsec:deep-learning}

The multilayer perceptron is the deep baseline. Stacked nonlinear layers
approximate arbitrary functions over engineered features and capture
higher-order feature interactions beyond the reach of linear models, but the
absence of any structural prior makes the perceptron data-hungry and its learned
weights opaque~\cite{cherfi2025mlp, zhao2025research}. It improves
discriminative power at a direct cost in interpretability.

Convolutional networks introduce a spatial prior. Applied in one dimension over
packet, byte, or flow sequences, convolutional filters extract local patterns
through weight sharing and translation invariance, which yields parameter
efficiency relative to a fully connected network of comparable
capacity~\cite{kumar2024deep}. The trade is a limited receptive field, so local
motifs are captured well but long-range temporal context is not. Recurrent
networks supply that missing temporal prior: gated variants, principally the
long short-term memory and gated recurrent unit, model long-range dependencies
across event sequences and so capture the progression of an attack over time,
at the cost of sequential computation that resists
parallelisation~\cite{devendiran2024dugat}. Hybrid convolutional-recurrent
models compose the two priors, placing convolutional feature extraction ahead of
recurrent temporal modelling so that local structure and temporal dependency are
learned jointly~\cite{psychogyios2024deep}.

Two further families extend the design space at higher cost. Attention-based
transformers replace recurrence with self-attention, modelling global
dependencies across a sequence without a recurrent bottleneck and performing
strongly on long inputs, but the quadratic cost of attention and the large
parameter counts of these models strain constrained device budgets unless
inference is offloaded~\cite{ali2024empowering}. Graph neural networks encode the
network as a graph of devices and flows and learn topology-aware embeddings,
which suit the detection of lateral movement and structural anomalies that
feature-based models miss, although graph construction and message passing add
their own overhead~\cite{xu2024applying, zhong2024survey}.

\subsection{Transparency, Trust, and Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:transparency-trust}

Detection capability is necessary but not sufficient for trustworthy security. A
recurring tension runs through the families above: representational power and
transparency trade against each other, so the deep architectures that detect the
subtlest attacks are also the least able to explain their alerts, while the
classical models that explain themselves readily are the least
expressive~\cite{sharma2024explainable, mohale2025systematic}.
Fig.~\ref{fig:interpretability-tradeoff} places the model families of this
section on the interpretability and capacity plane, where the classical families
occupy the high-transparency region compatible with constrained, auditable
deployment and the deep families trade that transparency for representational
power. Explainability methods partially close this gap for deep models. Post-hoc
attribution such as feature-importance scoring and local surrogate explanation,
together with attention inspection, recovers an approximate account of which
inputs drove a prediction, but these explanations are estimates rather than the
model's own reasoning and can be unstable across similar
inputs~\cite{gaspar2024explainable, wali2025explainable}. Transparency obtained
this way is therefore a mitigation, not a substitute for an inherently
interpretable model.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/Picture8.png}
  \caption[Interpretability against representational capacity for AI model families]{Interpretability against representational capacity for the model families of Section~\ref{sec:ai-models}. Classical families (circles) combine high transparency with moderate capacity and fall within the region of trustworthy, auditable detection on constrained devices; deep families (squares) gain capacity at the cost of transparency and fall outside it.}
  \label{fig:interpretability-tradeoff}

A second, distinct trust property is calibration. Behavioural trust scoring
requires that a score behave as a faithful probability and not merely as a
ranking, so that a fixed threshold carries a consistent meaning across devices
and over time. Deep classifiers are frequently overconfident and require an
explicit calibration step, such as isotonic or temperature scaling, before their
outputs can support a principled
threshold~\cite{balanya2024adaptive, dong2025survey}.

The final property is reproducibility. A security decision that cannot be
reproduced cannot be independently audited, and reproducibility is far harder to
guarantee for accelerated deep-learning pipelines, whose floating-point
reduction orders vary across hardware, than for deterministic classical
pipelines that yield bitwise-identical outputs under a fixed
environment~\cite{semmelrock2025reproducibility, kapoor2024reforms}. This
reproducibility requirement, rather than raw accuracy alone, shapes the model
choices that make verifiable IIoT security possible.

\section{Learning-Based Security for Constrained IIoT}
\label{sec:bg-learning}

Signature-based and rule-based defences, which match traffic against catalogues
of known attack patterns, are poorly suited to industrial IoT. They cannot
recognise novel or zero-day behaviour, they require constant manual updating,
and their central deployment assumes a visibility over device traffic that
distributed industrial networks do not provide. Learning-based methods address
these gaps by modelling normal behaviour and flagging deviations from it, which
allows previously unseen attacks to be detected as anomalies rather than as
known signatures. Building on the model families of Section~\ref{sec:ai-models},
this section explains the specific learning methods on which the systems in this
work rest: federated learning as the training paradigm, a hybrid convolutional
and recurrent network as the detection engine, and threshold-based trust scoring
as the mechanism that converts a detection into an enforceable decision. The
concern throughout is with how these methods operate; their measured behaviour
is reported in Chapter~\ref{Chapter_5}.

\subsection{Federated Learning}
\label{subsec:bg-fl}

Conventional machine-learning pipelines are centralised: data is gathered from
its sources into a single repository where a model is trained. In distributed
industrial settings this arrangement is problematic on two counts. Transmitting
raw operational data to a central server consumes bandwidth that constrained
networks cannot spare, and it exposes sensitive process data to interception and
to privacy or confidentiality
concerns~\cite{lu2024federated, dritsas2025federated}.

Federated learning removes the need to centralise raw data. Each participating
device trains a local copy of a shared model on its own data and transmits only
the resulting model update, the adjusted parameters, to a coordinating
aggregator. The aggregator combines the updates from all participants into an
improved global model and returns it for the next round of local training. The
canonical aggregation rule, federated averaging, computes the global model as a
weighted average of the client updates, with each client weighted by the size of
its local dataset so that participants holding more data contribute
proportionally~\cite{amadi2026lightweight}. Because the raw data never leaves the
device on which it was generated, federated learning reduces both the
communication cost and the privacy exposure of collaborative training, which is
what makes it appropriate for distributed industrial deployments in which data is
sensitive and connectivity is limited~\cite{ali2024blockchain}.

Federated learning also aligns naturally with a tiered trust architecture. Local
model training occurs at or near the devices, while the aggregation and the
recording of accepted updates occur at a better-resourced tier, which mirrors the
separation between a fast device-facing layer and a coordinating layer developed
later in this work. The arrangement is not free of risk: the update channel
admits model poisoning by a malicious participant, and heterogeneous data across
sites slows convergence, which is one reason the provenance of accepted updates
must itself be recorded.

\subsection{Convolutional and Recurrent Modelling}
\label{subsec:bg-cnnlstm}

Of the families compared in Section~\ref{sec:ai-models}, the detection engine
used across the systems studied here is a hybrid that combines a
one-dimensional convolutional stage with a recurrent stage. The design is
motivated by the structure of industrial network traffic, which is a sequence of
flow records exhibiting two kinds of pattern at once. There are short-range,
local patterns, such as burst rates, packet-size distributions, and
protocol-flag combinations, and there are longer-range temporal dependencies
arising from periodic sensing, control loops, and attack behaviour that unfolds
over time.

A one-dimensional convolutional layer is well suited to the first kind. It
slides learnable filters across the feature sequence and extracts local patterns
efficiently, producing feature maps that summarise the salient short-range
structure of the input~\cite{ige2024state, arsalan20241d}. A pooling stage then
reduces the dimensionality of these feature maps, retaining the strongest
responses while discarding fine-grained detail, which lowers computational cost
and improves robustness to small shifts in the input. The convolutional and
pooling stages together act as a feature compressor, reducing the raw input to a
compact set of representative features. This compressed representation has,
however, no explicit memory of order beyond the span of the filters. A recurrent
layer supplies that memory. A Long Short-Term Memory network processes the
sequence step by step and maintains an internal state through gated connections
that allow it to retain relevant information across long spans while discarding
the irrelevant, which lets it model dependencies that a convolutional layer
alone cannot~\cite{krichen2025long}.

Placing these stages in sequence, convolutional feature extraction and pooling
followed by recurrent temporal modelling, produces an engine that captures both
the local signatures and the temporal evolution of industrial
traffic~\cite{bamber2025hybrid}, with a final classification stage mapping the
learned representation to a probability over the traffic
classes~\cite{amadi2026lightweight, amadi2026symmetric}. This
convolutional-recurrent pairing is the behavioural engine common to the systems
examined in the later chapters. Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn-lstm} shows the arrangement of
stages, and Table~\ref{tab:cnn-lstm-stages} summarises the function of each.

\subsection{Trust Scoring and Revocation}
\label{subsec:bg-trust}

Detection is only useful if it drives an action. Behavioural trust management,
in which a node's trustworthiness is quantified from its observed behaviour and
used to govern its participation, is an established response to the problem of
malicious or compromised nodes in distributed and industrial
networks~\cite{ferraris2024survey}. Rather than treating security as a single
admitted-or-blocked decision made once at authentication, such schemes maintain
a continuous, evolving measure of each node's reliability and adjust it as
behaviour is observed~\cite{ullah2024deep}.

In the systems studied here, this principle is realised through a defined
sequence, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:trust-flow}. The behavioural engine
produces, for the device or node whose traffic was classified, a set of class
probabilities across normal and anomalous categories. These are reduced to a
single trust score in the range $[0,1]$, which is then compared against a
predefined threshold $\tau$. A device whose score satisfies
$\text{score} \geq \tau$ is treated as trustworthy and its interactions proceed;
a device whose score falls below the threshold, $\text{score} < \tau$, is
flagged as anomalous. Threshold-based conversion of a continuous anomaly signal
into a discrete security decision of this kind is a common and well-studied
mechanism in network anomaly detection~\cite{komadina2024comparing}, and it is
meaningful only when the score is calibrated in the sense of
Section~\ref{subsec:transparency-trust}.

A flagged device is not merely logged. It is moved through a defined lifecycle
toward isolation, in which its interactions are restricted or quarantined, and
its revocation is then recorded so that other participants no longer extend
trust to it. Anchoring trust and revocation decisions on a distributed ledger is
a recognised approach to making them tamper-evident and consistent across a
decentralised network, since no single participant can unilaterally reinstate a
revoked node~\cite{d2025blockchain}. Once the revocation is written as an
immutable ledger record, the event is propagated to the other nodes, which
update their trust state accordingly. In the present systems, this
ledger-recorded and broadcast revocation is what prevents a node excluded at one
point from silently re-entering elsewhere~\cite{amadi2026symmetric}. This
coupling, from class-probability output, to a trust score, to a threshold
comparison, and then to a ledger-recorded and propagated revocation, is the
mechanism by which learning-based detection becomes an enforceable trust
decision. Table~\ref{tab:trust-stages} summarises the role of each stage.

constitutes the learning-based security foundation on which the proposed
architecture is built.

\section{Blockchain and Distributed Ledgers for IIoT}
\label{sec:blockchain-ledgers}

The behavioural trust decisions produced by the learning engines of
Section~\ref{sec:bg-learning} are only as dependable as the record that
preserves them: a trust score, an alert, or a revocation event that can be
altered after the fact cannot support accountable, auditable security in a
mission-critical setting. A distributed ledger supplies that record. A
blockchain is a distributed, append-only ledger in which transactions are
grouped into blocks, and each block is bound to its predecessor by a
cryptographic hash, so that any later modification is exposed by recomputation
rather than concealed behind trust in a
custodian~\cite{xu2024exploring, savadatti2025survey}. The property that matters
for constrained industrial deployments is not currency or decentralised finance
but tamper-evident provenance: an immutable, independently checkable trail of
the security-relevant events that a regulator or a partner institution must be
able to reconstruct. Realising that property within a real device budget depends
on three design choices, namely how the ledger is structured, who is permitted
to write to it, and how distributed validators agree on its contents.

\subsection{Blocks, Hashing, and Immutability}
\label{subsec:blocks-hashing}

A block is a batch of transactions preceded by a header that records, among
other fields, the cryptographic hash of the previous block. Chaining blocks by
hash makes the ledger tamper-evident: altering any committed transaction changes
its block hash, which invalidates the reference held by every subsequent block,
so a single edit is detectable by recomputing the
chain~\cite{kuznetsov2024merkle}. Within a block, the transactions are
summarised by a Merkle tree whose root commits to the entire set, allowing the
membership of one transaction to be proven in logarithmic time without
transmitting the whole block. Immutability is therefore a joint property of
hash-linking and replication: the more independent validators that hold and
verify the chain, the harder it becomes to rewrite history unilaterally, and the
cheaper it is for any party to detect an attempt.

For constrained IIoT the cost of this guarantee is the binding concern. Full
replication and continuous verification exceed the memory and energy budgets of
low-class devices, and storing raw industrial payloads on-chain is infeasible at
operational data rates. The standard resolution is to keep payloads off-chain
and commit only their cryptographic digests to the ledger, so that integrity is
certified without the ledger bearing the data volume~\cite{eren2025security}.

\subsection{Permissioned and Permissionless Ledgers, and Smart Contracts}
\label{subsec:permissioned-contracts}

Ledgers differ in who may append to them. A permissionless ledger admits
anonymous participants and resists censorship, but it pays for open membership
with limited throughput and probabilistic finality, in which a committed block is
only ever overwhelmingly, never certainly, final. A permissioned ledger restricts
write access to a vetted set of validators, which yields higher throughput and
deterministic finality at the cost of a narrower trust base. For constrained,
mission-critical IIoT, the permissioned model is the natural fit, because an
industrial consortium has identifiable members, contractual accountability, and
regulatory obligations that an anonymous network cannot satisfy, and because
deterministic finality aligns with the timing guarantees such systems
require~\cite{lahbib2024blockchain, bobde2024enhancing}.

Smart contracts extend a ledger from a passive record into an active policy
layer. A smart contract is self-executing code stored on-chain and run
identically by every validator, so its outcome is deterministic and
independently verifiable rather than dependent on any single
party~\cite{wu2024comprehensive, wei2024survey}. This is the mechanism by which
behavioural policies such as a trust threshold, a revocation rule, or an access
decision can be enforced on-chain: the same logic that scores a device can be
encoded as contract logic that records, and acts on, the resulting verdict
without a trusted intermediary. The contract does not improve the accuracy of
the decision; it makes the decision and its consequences auditable.

\subsection{Consensus Families under Resource Constraints}
\label{subsec:consensus-families}

Consensus is the mechanism by which distributed validators agree on a single
canonical ledger state despite faults and adversaries, and the families in use
differ sharply in cost and finality. Proof-of-Work secures agreement through a
computational puzzle and offers only probabilistic finality at very high energy
cost. Proof-of-Stake replaces computation with economic stake, which lowers
energy demand but presupposes a token economy. The Byzantine-fault-tolerant
family, exemplified by Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, delivers
deterministic finality and tolerates a bounded fraction of Byzantine validators,
but its all-to-all messaging scales quadratically and so limits the practical
validator-set size. Proof-of-Authority dispenses with both puzzles and stake: a
small set of vetted authorities produce blocks in a fixed or rotating order,
achieving deterministic finality at negligible energy cost~\cite{jain2025survey}.

membership, finality, and fitness for a constrained industrial ledger, and shows
why the choice narrows toward authority-based consensus once energy cost and
deterministic finality are treated as hard requirements rather than preferences.

determinism. Byzantine-fault-tolerant consensus restores determinism but bounds
the validator set through its messaging cost. Proof-of-Authority therefore
emerges as the practical basis for a permissioned industrial ledger, subject to
one weakness that the next subsection addresses: a static authority set assumes
its authorities remain both honest and available.

\subsection{Reliability-Aware Consensus: PoA\textsuperscript{2} and SAM+}
\label{subsec:poa2-sam}

Static Proof-of-Authority treats every authority as permanently trustworthy, so
a single compromised or offline authority degrades both liveness and safety.
Reliability-aware consensus removes that assumption by making authority dynamic.
Proof-of-Authority-and-Association augments each validator's fixed authority
with a reliability score that reflects its recent operational consistency: a
block is accepted once the weighted signatures of the active validators cross an
acceptance threshold, and validators are promoted from or demoted to a standby
set according to a moving average of their signing history, with a gap condition
between the least reliable active and the most reliable standby validator
triggering replacement~\cite{kim2025proof}. The result is a self-healing
validator set that tolerates degraded authorities without surrendering the
deterministic finality that constrained, mission-critical operation requires. A
companion mechanism, Smart Auto-Mining Plus (SAM+), regulates block production
by adjusting the mining difficulty to the observed network load, so that the
fast layer neither idles under light traffic nor saturates its validators during
bursts of updates~\cite{purechain_wp}.

These mechanisms operate over a layered permissioned ledger whose structure
separates high-frequency operational commitments from the low-frequency,
high-value records that must remain externally checkable.
Fig.~\ref{fig:purechain-layers} shows that layered organisation as a four-stage
pipeline: constrained clients emit signed model-update metadata; a fast
validation layer absorbs high-frequency updates with lightweight checks; the
PoA\textsuperscript{2} consensus layer enforces signature, timestamp, and
access-control verification to reach deterministic agreement; and an immutable
anchor layer preserves only the finalised, low-frequency records that must
remain externally auditable. Reading the pipeline from left to right makes the
division of labour explicit: frequent operations are absorbed where they are
cheap, while the anchor layer is reserved for the few events whose external
verifiability justifies their cost.

\section{Hybrid Ledger Architectures and Trust Partitioning}
\label{sec:hybrid-ledgers}

The permissioned ledger of Section~\ref{sec:blockchain-ledgers}, secured by
reliability-aware consensus, delivers the fast and deterministic finality that
constrained industrial operation requires, but it confines trust to the members
of the consortium: an external regulator or partner institution must ultimately
take the consortium's word for the integrity of its own records. A single public
ledger inverts the problem, offering verification that rests on no single
institution while being unable to absorb high-frequency industrial event rates
within a realistic device budget. No single ledger satisfies both the
speed-and-cost constraint and the external-verifiability constraint at once.
Hybrid ledger architectures resolve the tension by combining more than one
ledger, and the principle that governs how work is divided between them is trust
partitioning: each operation is placed on the layer whose trust model and cost
profile match the operation's frequency and value.

\subsection{The Hybrid Ledger Pattern}
\label{subsec:hybrid-pattern}

The hybrid pattern pairs a fast permissioned layer with a public anchoring
layer. The permissioned layer records the bulk of operational activity, while
the public layer receives only periodic, compact commitments that bind the
permissioned state to an externally verifiable record. The scaling literature
formalises this as a Layer-1 and Layer-2 relationship, in which a Layer-2
batches many operations and commits a single proof or root to a Layer-1 chain,
so that the committed state inherits Layer-1 security at a small fraction of its
per-operation cost~\cite{rebello2024survey}. The arrangement need not sit within
one system: cross-chain and interoperability mechanisms, including relays,
notary schemes, and hash-locking, move commitments between heterogeneous ledgers
that were never designed to interoperate~\cite{hassan2025sok}.
Table~\ref{tab:ledger-types} contrasts the public, permissioned, and hybrid
options on the properties that decide where an operation belongs.

Across these variants the recurring idea is separation by frequency and value:
operations that occur often are kept where they are cheap, and records that must
be trusted by outsiders are committed where outsiders can check them. The
pattern is therefore defined less by a particular technology than by the
placement decision it enforces.

\subsection{Trust Partitioning as an Architectural Principle}
\label{subsec:trust-partitioning}

Read only as a performance optimisation, the fast-plus-anchor split is a caching
argument. Read as a trust argument, it is more consequential. The high-frequency
behavioural operations of a constrained deployment, such as telemetry,
model-update metadata, and intra-consortium validation, carry a trust
requirement that the known, vetted validators of a permissioned layer already
satisfy, so placing them there sacrifices nothing that an external party needs.
The low-frequency, high-value events, such as audit roots, revocation records,
and cross-institution commitments, carry a trust requirement that the consortium
cannot satisfy on its own account, and only a genuinely public anchor makes them
checkable by a party outside the consortium~\cite{wang2024public}. Trust
partitioning is the discipline of routing each class of event to the layer that
can actually vouch for it.

This layered organisation is not merely convenient. Layered and tiered
blockchain designs have been analysed as a structural response to the resource
and trust asymmetries of industrial
deployments~\cite{li2025blockchain, song2024novel}, and the structural necessity
of such a reference architecture is argued in the systematic review that
scaffolds this work~\cite{amadi2026security}, which Chapter~\ref{Chapter_3}
extends. What the principle does not by itself settle is whether a single
partitioning rule can hold across different ledger substrates; that question is
taken up in Section~\ref{subsec:hybrid-instances}.

\subsection{Instances Across Ledger Substrates}
\label{subsec:hybrid-instances}

The same partitioning principle admits realisations that differ in substrate.
Within a single hybrid ledger, a permissioned network-service layering can absorb
high-frequency validation while a public augmented Layer-1 preserves the
anchored record, so the split is internal to one design. Across two distinct
ledgers, a permissioned Hyperledger Fabric network can carry operational
transactions while a public Ethereum network holds the anchoring commitments, so
the split is a boundary between technologies. Across compute tiers, a federated
edge-and-cloud arrangement can partition work by where it is produced and where
it must be preserved.

Presented here only as building blocks, these realisations establish three
facts: that the pattern exists, that it is a partitioning of trust rather than
merely of load, and that more than one substrate can carry it. Whether they
express a single, substrate-independent trust-partitioning principle is a
question this section deliberately leaves open. Fig.~\ref{fig:hybrid-pattern}
shows the pattern in its generic form, with fast permissioned layers feeding
periodic commitments to a public anchor.

\section{Post-Quantum Cryptography and Device Identity}
\label{sec:bg-pqc}

Every mechanism described so far rests, in the end, on public-key cryptography.
Device credentials authenticate the clients whose updates train the behavioural
model, signatures bind validators to the blocks they endorse, and key exchange
protects the channels over which commitments travel. The durability of the
architecture is therefore bounded by the durability of these primitives, and
that durability can no longer be assumed. This section sets out the quantum
threat to classical public-key schemes, the primitives standardised to replace
them together with their cost, and the identity and key lifecycle through which
a constrained device must carry those primitives.

\subsection{The Quantum Threat to Classical Public-Key Cryptography}
\label{subsec:bg-quantum-threat}

The asymmetric schemes that underpin current authentication and key exchange,
namely RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, derive their security from integer
factorisation and discrete logarithms, problems that a sufficiently capable
quantum computer could solve efficiently using Shor's
algorithm~\cite{shor1999polynomial}. Symmetric primitives are affected far less,
since the best known quantum attack on them yields only a quadratic speed-up that
longer keys absorb, so the burden of migration falls almost entirely on
public-key operations~\cite{wang2025review}. The risk, moreover, is not deferred
to the arrival of such a machine. Under the harvest-now, decrypt-later model, an
adversary can capture encrypted traffic today and decrypt it once the capability
matures, placing data with a long confidentiality horizon at present
risk~\cite{kagai2025harvest, singh2024managing}. Industrial equipment compounds
this exposure, because devices fielded today may remain in service, and remain
trusted, for a decade or more, so the effective deadline for migration is set by
the lifetime of the data and the device rather than by the arrival of the
machine. These pressures led the United States National Institute of Standards
and Technology to finalise its first post-quantum standards in August 2024,
concluding a process begun in 2016~\cite{fips203, fips204, fips205}.

\subsection{Lattice- and Hash-Based Replacements and Their Cost}
\label{subsec:bg-pqc-primitives}

The three standards divide the work of the classical schemes they replace.
FIPS~203 specifies ML-KEM, a key-encapsulation mechanism derived from
CRYSTALS-Kyber, whose security rests on the Module Learning With Errors problem:
recovering a secret from a system of linear equations over polynomial rings that
has been deliberately perturbed by small noise, a task believed to be hard for
classical and quantum computers alike~\cite{fips203}. FIPS~204 specifies ML-DSA,
a digital-signature scheme built on the same module-lattice
foundation~\cite{fips204}, and FIPS~205 specifies SLH-DSA, a stateless
signature scheme whose security relies only on the properties of hash functions,
offering the most conservative assumptions at the price of large
signatures~\cite{fips205}.

The cost of this resilience is paid chiefly in size. Table~\ref{tab:pqc-sizes}
and Fig.~\ref{fig:pqc-sizes} compare the public keys, ciphertexts, and
signatures of the standardised schemes with the classical schemes they replace.
An ML-KEM-512 public key occupies 800~bytes and its ciphertext 768~bytes, against
32~bytes each for X25519 key agreement, while an ML-DSA-44 signature occupies
2{,}420~bytes against 64~bytes for ECDSA over P-256. Benchmarking on consumer and
industrial IoT hardware indicates that the lattice arithmetic itself is
tractable on modern microcontrollers, and that the larger artefacts instead
surface as transmission, fragmentation, and storage overheads that weigh most
heavily on memory- and bandwidth-limited
devices~\cite{fitzgibbon2024constrained, hanna2025comprehensive,
cruz2025measuring}. On low-power links such as IEEE~802.15.4, whose physical
frame is limited to 127~bytes~\cite{ieee802154}, a single ML-KEM ciphertext must
be fragmented across several frames, so every retransmission and every lost
fragment multiplies the cost. Reducing what crosses the link, rather than
accelerating the arithmetic, is accordingly the principal lever for post-quantum
security on constrained devices~\cite{castiglione2024integrating}.

\subsection{Device Identity and the Key Lifecycle}
\label{subsec:bg-identity}

A cryptographic primitive protects nothing until it is bound to an identity and
carried through a lifecycle. For a constrained industrial device that lifecycle
comprises the derivation of an identity, the issuance of credentials and keys
bound to it, their use in authentication and key establishment, their periodic
rotation, and, when trust is lost, their revocation. Table~\ref{tab:key-lifecycle}
summarises the stages and the concern that dominates each.

The first stage is the most easily neglected. A key is only as unpredictable as
the randomness from which it was generated, and constrained devices differ widely
in the entropy available to them, from dedicated true random number generators in
secure elements, through physically unclonable functions that derive
device-unique responses from manufacturing variation~\cite{hao2025efficient}, to
the noise present in sensor readings and environmental measurements.
Entropy-seeded identity derivation combines such sources so that a device
identity is both unique and difficult to forge, and pairs it with compressed
post-quantum key delivery so that the larger artefacts of
Section~\ref{subsec:bg-pqc-primitives} can be carried over constrained
links~\cite{amadi2025quantum}. The final stage connects identity to the
mechanisms of the preceding sections. Revocation is the point at which the
behavioural evidence of Section~\ref{subsec:bg-trust} acts on a cryptographic
credential, and the ledger of Section~\ref{sec:blockchain-ledgers} is the means
by which that withdrawal becomes visible to every party that relies on it.

\section{The Case for Trust-Partitioned Security}
\label{sec:case}

The security of a constrained industrial deployment cannot be delegated to any
single mechanism. Section~\ref{sec:bg-landscape} showed that the threat surface
is broad and its consequences physical rather than merely informational.
Sections~\ref{sec:ai-models} and~\ref{sec:bg-learning} showed that behavioural
detection can be made accurate, calibrated, and auditable, but only within a
model whose cost and transparency fit the device that must run it.
Section~\ref{sec:blockchain-ledgers} showed that a permissioned ledger under
reliability-aware consensus can render those decisions tamper-evident at a
deterministic and affordable cost. Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-ledgers} showed that
a hybrid ledger, governed by trust partitioning, can reconcile the speed a device
can afford with the external verifiability a regulator or partner institution
demands. Section~\ref{sec:bg-pqc} showed that the cryptographic anchor beneath
all of these must migrate to primitives whose larger artefacts press on the same
budgets. Each of these is necessary; none is sufficient on its own.

Read together, they converge on a single requirement: security for constrained,
mission-critical IIoT must be at once behavioural, verifiable, and affordable.
Behavioural, because static signatures cannot anticipate the attacks that now
matter. Verifiable, because a decision that cannot be independently
reconstructed cannot be trusted by the parties who bear its consequences.
Affordable, because a mechanism that exceeds the device budget is never
deployed, whatever its merits on paper. Trust partitioning is the principle that
holds these three in tension without sacrificing any of them, by routing each
operation to the layer that can both bear its cost and vouch for its trust, so
that speed is not bought at the price of verifiability nor verifiability at the
price of feasibility.

A fourth requirement is temporal, and it is easily overlooked. The identities
and commitments that anchor an industrial system are long-lived, so an adversary
need not break current cryptography today in order to defeat it, as
Section~\ref{subsec:bg-quantum-threat} established~\cite{singh2024managing}. The
standardisation of post-quantum key encapsulation and signatures makes migration
possible; what makes it hard is that lattice-based schemes carry keys and
ciphertexts an order of magnitude larger than the elliptic-curve schemes they
replace, pressing on exactly the memory and transmission budgets that already
constrain these devices~\cite{castiglione2024integrating}. Post-quantum
resistance is thus not a separate concern bolted onto the architecture but
another face of the same affordability problem: the cryptographic anchor of
trust must be quantum-resistant and compact at once.

Beneath all four requirements runs the resource budget, which is less a
requirement than a filter applied to every other choice. It is the budget that
rules out proof-of-work, that forbids unbounded validator sets, that penalises
opaque high-capacity models, and that turns post-quantum migration from a
configuration change into a design problem. A proposal that sets the budget aside
is not conservative but inapplicable, and much of the apparent disagreement
across the mechanisms surveyed here dissolves once the budget is treated as
binding rather than negotiable.

Taken together, these requirements describe a target that none of the
mechanisms in isolation reaches: a security architecture that is behavioural in
its detection, tamper-evident in its record, trust-partitioned in its structure,
post-quantum in its cryptographic anchor, and viable within a real device budget
throughout. The case for trust-partitioned security is the case that these
properties are jointly necessary and jointly attainable, rather than a menu from
which one selects. Whether existing practice already offers such an
architecture, and if it does not, precisely where and why it falls short, cannot
be settled from the selective reading of a background chapter. It calls for the
systematic, reproducible review undertaken in Chapter~\ref{Chapter_3}, where the
distance between these requirements and what current approaches deliver is
measured and the resulting gap made precise.
