\label{Chapter_3}

This chapter measures the distance between the requirements established in
Chapter~\ref{Chapter_2} and what existing work delivers. It first sets out the
systematic review protocol and the corpus it produced, then positions that
corpus against prior reviews of the field, examines the state of the art across
the three technical capabilities on which a trust-partitioned architecture
depends, and closes by consolidating the gaps that remain and the research
questions they motivate.

\section{Methodology for Review of Literature}
\label{sec:review-method}

A defensible account of where existing security architectures fall short of the
requirements set out in Section~\ref{sec:case} cannot rest on a selective reading
of convenient sources. It requires a search that is reproducible, criteria that
are stated in advance, and a corpus assembled without the bias that undermines a
narrative survey. The review reported in this chapter therefore adopts the
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)
framework~\cite{prisma}, which prescribes a transparent, stage-by-stage
procedure for identifying, screening, and selecting studies and for reporting the
exclusions made at each stage. The protocol, search, and corpus extend the
systematic review published in~\cite{amadi2026security}. The phenomenon of
interest is the intersection at which the thesis is positioned: learning-driven
detection, blockchain-anchored provenance, and post-quantum protection, examined
under the resource constraints of industrial deployment rather than in the
abstract.

The search was conducted across five databases, namely IEEE Xplore, Scopus, Web
of Science, the ACM Digital Library, and SpringerLink, between 1 March and
5 April 2025, targeting publications from 2020 to 2025. Restricting the corpus to
this window is deliberate rather than incidental: the convergence of hybrid
ledgers, learning-based security, and post-quantum standardisation that the
thesis addresses emerged substantially within this period, so an earlier window
would dilute relevance rather than improve coverage. Six core terms structured
the query, namely ``hybrid blockchain'', ``digital twin'', ``data-sharing
security'', ``cross-chain'', ``privacy-preserving blockchain'', and
``energy-efficient consensus'', each adapted to the query syntax of the
individual databases. The terms centre on digital-twin ecosystems deliberately.
A digital twin mirrors industrial devices and processes through continuous,
high-frequency, multi-party data exchange, which makes the digital-twin
ecosystem the most demanding instance of the industrial data-sharing problem
that constrained IIoT security must solve, and the setting in which hybrid
ledgers have been most extensively proposed. Table~\ref{tab:search-strings}
records the exact string issued to each database, so that the search can be
reproduced rather than merely described.

\subsection{Quality Assessment and Bias Evaluation}
\label{subsec:review-quality}

Each study that passed full-text eligibility was assessed against the five
quality criteria QC1 to QC5 of Table~\ref{tab:review-criteria}: clarity of
research objectives; reproducibility of the proposed method or system design;
traceability of results, meaning whether performance claims such as throughput,
latency, or energy efficiency were supported by experimental data or formal
analysis; relevance to hybrid blockchain and resource-aware data sharing; and
acknowledgement of study-level limitations. Failure on objectives,
reproducibility, or relevance was grounds for exclusion at full-text review.

Five sources of bias were addressed directly rather than left implicit.
Publication bias was mitigated by spanning five databases across multiple
publishers and venues. Language bias is acknowledged, since the English-only
restriction may omit relevant non-English work, although English dominates
peer-reviewed research in this area over the target period. Selection bias was
controlled by applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria consistently across
both screening phases, with ambiguous cases resolved by re-evaluation. Recency
bias is inherent to the 2020 to 2025 scope but deliberate, given that the field
emerged largely within it. Aggregation bias, arising from heterogeneous
performance metrics across studies, is acknowledged as a residual limitation:
every quantitative figure carried into this review is attributed to its primary
source and is not treated as a cross-study validated or normalised estimate.

\section{Review of Recent Works}
\label{sec:recent-works}

Substantial effort has been directed at securing blockchain-enabled and
digital-twin systems, and a first orientation is best obtained not from
individual proposals but from the reviews that have already synthesised them.
Table~\ref{tab:review-comparison} compares the most relevant prior review and
survey works along the dimensions that decide whether a review speaks to the
problem addressed here: whether it treats the digital-twin ecosystem (DTE) as its
object, whether it engages conventional blockchain technology (CBT) or the hybrid
model (HBT), whether it organises its findings through a three-dimensional
lifecycle-layer-security taxonomy, whether it performs a comparative defence-gap
analysis, and whether it traces each open issue to a research direction. The
final three columns tell the decisive story at a glance: across every prior
review, the taxonomy, defence-gap, and issue-to-direction columns are uniformly
absent, and only the review reported here supplies all three.

The prior reviews fall into three groups, each capable within its remit and each
limited in the same structural way. The first group treats blockchain as a broad
enabler of digital twins without isolating the hybrid model as a necessary
architectural response. Surveys of blockchain for digital twins~\cite{yaqoob}, of
blockchain-based digital-twin trends~\cite{sabah}, of digital-twin and blockchain
integration~\cite{hemdan}, and the more recent multi-domain
accounts~\cite{dwivedi, Nexus} cover the pairing of the two technologies capably,
but treat the choice between public, private, and hybrid ledgers as a design
detail rather than as the structural pivot on which digital-twin security turns.
The second group narrows to the hybrid model but not to the digital-twin setting:
the one prior systematic review of hybrid blockchain platforms~\cite{alkhateeb}
addresses general Internet-of-Things systems and does not engage the
lifecycle-specific trade-offs that arise under real-time, high-frequency
workloads. The third group reviews digital-twin architecture and enabling
technologies thoroughly, in accounts of digital-twin technology and
networking~\cite{maggie}, of enabling technologies and prospects~\cite{Mihai},
and of Internet-of-Digital-Twins architecture~\cite{wangbest}, but offers no
security-focused synthesis, no defence-gap analysis, and no structured mapping
from open issue to future direction.

A closer reading within these groups reinforces the point. The domain-specific
reviews demonstrate real depth in their settings, of digital twins in
remanufacturing supply chains~\cite{chen}, of blockchain for secure health data
sharing~\cite{peng, upadrista}, of building-lifecycle management~\cite{adu}, of
security in Industry~4.0 digital twins~\cite{elhajj}, and of data fusion in the
energy sector~\cite{jiang}, yet their security treatment stays bound to the
application rather than abstracted into a reusable architectural principle. The
recent multi-domain reviews extend coverage and currency, and one adds
case-study evidence alongside its systematic method~\cite{christos, Nexus}, but
none introduces the three analytical dimensions on which a trust-partitioned
architecture must be assessed. Several of these works are systematic in
method~\cite{chen, alkhateeb, upadrista, adu, christos, Nexus, elhajj, jiang},
which confirms that methodological rigour is not what the field lacks; what it
lacks is a security-focused synthesis organised around the hybrid,
trust-partitioned model.

Read together, these reviews establish that the field has matured and that the
pairing of blockchain with digital-twin and industrial systems is now well
surveyed. What none provides, as the three rightmost columns of
Table~\ref{tab:review-comparison} make explicit, is a security-focused synthesis
that isolates the hybrid, trust-partitioned model as a structural response,
measures the defence coverage it achieves, and traces the residual gaps to the
directions that would close them. That absence defines the contribution of the
review reported here. The substantive state of the art on which it draws is
examined next in Section~\ref{sec:soa}, before the resulting gaps are
consolidated in Section~\ref{sec:review-gap}.

\section{State-of-the-Art Approaches}
\label{sec:soa}

Three technical capabilities carry the weight of the reviewed corpus: detecting
compromise as it occurs, recording that detection in a form that cannot later be
altered, and protecting the record against an adversary whose capabilities grow
over time. The approaches that address each are surveyed in turn, with attention
to what they achieve and to the limitation that each leaves for the next to
resolve. Because the field has continued to advance since the systematic search
closed, this section draws both on the retained corpus and on studies published
after the search window, so that the gaps consolidated in
Section~\ref{sec:review-gap} reflect the current state of the art rather than its
position at a single date. Table~\ref{tab:soa-summary} summarises the approaches
discussed and the limitation each leaves open.

\subsection{Learning-Based Detection and Trust Scoring}
\label{subsec:soa-learning}

Learning-based detection has displaced signature matching as the dominant
approach to industrial intrusion detection, because the attacks that matter in
operational technology rarely match a known signature and often manifest only as
a drift in behaviour. The reviewed corpus divides along the representational
depth of the model, and the division carries the familiar trade-off between
interpretability and capacity into concrete system designs.

Classical machine-learning detectors remain widely deployed for industrial
traffic, where tree ensembles and margin-based classifiers operating on
engineered flow features deliver strong accuracy at a computational cost that
constrained gateways can absorb, and with decision logic an operator can
inspect~\cite{majidian2024optimizing}. Their weakness is equally documented:
performance depends on the quality of hand-engineered features, degrades under
the distribution drift that characterises evolving industrial networks, and
falls away on temporally extended or previously unseen attack patterns that the
feature set was not designed to capture~\cite{agate2024driftaware,
ennaji2025adversarial}.

Deep detectors raise the achievable ceiling by learning representations directly
from traffic. Convolutional detectors extract spatial structure from packet and
flow encodings~\cite{alshehri2024selfattention, hassini2024endtoend}; recurrent
detectors built on long short-term memory and gated recurrent units model the
temporal dependence of an attack that unfolds across a
session~\cite{chen2024xgboostlstm, raeiszadeh2024realtime}; and hybrid
convolutional-recurrent detectors combine the two to capture spatiotemporal
behaviour that neither recovers alone~\cite{chen2025cnnlstm,
anwer2025federated}. More expressive still, graph-based detectors exploit the
topology of an industrial network to localise
compromise~\cite{villegas2025dynamic, deng2025edgefeatured}, and attention-based
models extend the temporal reach of detection across longer
horizons~\cite{akuthota2025transformer, ishtiaq2025cstafnet}. The recurring
limitation across this family is not accuracy but cost and accountability:
higher-capacity models demand memory, energy, and labelled data that constrained
deployments cannot always supply, and their decisions resist the explanation and
the bitwise reproducibility that an audited setting requires.

A parallel strand addresses the distribution of training itself. Federated
detectors keep raw industrial data on the premises that generate it, exchanging
only model updates, which answers the confidentiality and data-sovereignty
constraints of multi-site industrial operation~\cite{lee2025lightweight,
mao2025fedinnid}. The approach introduces its own exposure, since the update
channel invites model poisoning and the heterogeneity of industrial data across
sites degrades convergence~\cite{quyen2025fedkd, sun2025personalized}. A further
strand recognises that a detection verdict is only actionable if its confidence
is trustworthy, and applies calibration and explanation so that a reported
probability reflects an observed rate and a flagged event can be
justified~\cite{shoukat2025trustids, pal2025calibrated}; yet such work rarely
extends to making the calibrated verdict independently reproducible or
externally checkable.

These strands converge on behavioural trust scoring, the thread most relevant to
the architecture developed later. Rather than emitting a one-shot alert,
trust-scoring approaches maintain a continuous, decaying estimate of a device's
or a peer's reliability, updated from observed behaviour and used to gate access
or trigger revocation~\cite{anwer2025tead, awan2025stacktrust}. Reputation and
trust-management schemes formalise how such scores accrue, decay, and drive
revocation across a network of interacting
nodes~\cite{bampatsikos2025trustscore, wang2025continuous}. The limitation that
unites the entire subsection is now visible: learning-based detection can be
made accurate, temporally aware, privacy-preserving, and even calibrated, but the
trust verdict it produces is computed and stored wherever the model runs, so it
remains exactly as mutable as its host. A verdict that can be altered after the
fact cannot anchor an accountable security decision, which is the requirement
that ledger-based provenance, examined in Section~\ref{subsec:soa-ledger}, exists
to meet.

\subsection{Blockchain and Consensus for IIoT Provenance}
\label{subsec:soa-ledger}

The limitation that closed the previous subsection, a trust verdict as mutable as
the host that computes it, is precisely what distributed-ledger provenance
exists to remove. The reviewed corpus applies blockchain to industrial systems
less as a currency than as an integrity layer, committing security-relevant
events to a record that no single participant can silently revise. The concrete
systems divide by the trust model of the ledger they build on and by the
consensus that keeps that ledger consistent.

Permissioned ledgers dominate the industrial setting, because a vetted
consortium supplies the accountable membership and the throughput that
anonymous public chains cannot. Systems in this class anchor device identities,
access decisions, and audit records on a consortium chain, giving an external
party a tamper-evident trail to reconstruct~\cite{samriya2025workload,
salam2026secure}, and several couple the ledger directly to intrusion detection
so that a flagged event is committed rather than merely
logged~\cite{song2025hybrid, peng2025bctdics}. Their recurring cost is
throughput and storage: committing at industrial event rates strains both the
consensus path and the on-chain footprint, so raw telemetry cannot be placed on
the ledger without exhausting the very budgets that constrain the
devices~\cite{xiong2025sharding, ding2025dcstorage}.

Consensus is where the resource tension concentrates. The energy and latency of
proof-of-work rule it out for constrained operation, which has driven industrial
designs toward Byzantine-fault-tolerant and authority-based protocols that reach
deterministic finality at a fraction of the cost~\cite{zheng2025trbft,
wu2025dbpbft}. Reputation-aware and reliability-weighted consensus variants
extend this by adjusting a validator's influence according to its observed
behaviour, so that a degraded or compromised authority loses weight without
halting the network~\cite{zeng2025ibft, xiong2025reputation}. Even these,
however, assume a validator set small enough to coordinate, which bounds the
scale at which a single permissioned ledger can operate and leaves open how
high-frequency operation and externally verifiable finality are to be reconciled
at once.

A further body of work turns the ledger from a passive record into an active
control point through smart contracts, encoding access policy, revocation, and
audit logic that executes identically across validators~\cite{li2025hyperiiot,
dildar2025endtoend}. This is the mechanism by which a trust decision from the
detection layer can be made not only recorded but enforced, and by which
revocation can be triggered without a trusted intermediary~\cite{he2026bfldias,
xia2026ecroa}. The limitation that unites the subsection is one of placement
rather than capability: a single ledger that is fast enough for operational
commitment is rarely public enough for external verification, and one that is
public enough for verification is rarely fast enough for operational commitment.
Resolving that tension is the concern of the hybrid and tiered designs examined
in Section~\ref{subsec:soa-hybrid}.

\subsection{Hybrid Ledgers and Post-Quantum Protection}
\label{subsec:soa-hybrid}

The placement tension that closed the previous subsection is the problem that
hybrid and tiered ledger designs set out to dissolve rather than merely trade
away. The reviewed corpus resolves it not by choosing between the two ledger
types but by composing them, assigning high-frequency operation to a fast
permissioned layer and externally checkable finality to a slower public anchor,
with a defined path between the two.

Tiered and layered architectures place a permissioned ledger close to the
devices, where consensus is cheap and finality is quick, and periodically commit
a compact digest of that ledger's state to a second, more widely trusted
chain~\cite{ullah2025mbid, banitalebi2025edblsd}. The mechanism that joins the
layers is cross-chain anchoring: a hash or Merkle root computed on the fast layer
is written to the anchor chain, so that a compact commitment on the public ledger
stands in for the full history held below, and any later dispute can be settled
against it without replaying the whole record~\cite{oleszkiewicz2026cross,
correa2026decoupling}. This composition is what allows a single system to
satisfy operational speed and external verifiability at once, and it is the
structural pattern on which a trust-partitioned architecture depends. Its cost is
added coordination and the security of the bridge itself, since a compromised or
poorly designed cross-chain link becomes the weakest point of the combined
system~\cite{zhou2025bridgeguard, mukherjee2025double}.

A verifiability that is sound today is not thereby sound for the lifetime of an
industrial deployment, and this is where the second concern of the subsection
enters. The cryptographic signatures and hashes that make a ledger
tamper-evident rest on assumptions that a cryptographically relevant quantum
computer would overturn, and the harvest-now, decrypt-later posture means that
records anchored today can be targeted once such capability
exists~\cite{reddy2025quantum, revathi2025enhancing}. Work in this area migrates
ledger signatures and identity credentials to lattice-based and other
quantum-resistant primitives, and reports the throughput and footprint
consequences of doing so on the ledger path~\cite{hussain2025quantum,
hao2025efficient}. The unresolved difficulty, anticipated in
Section~\ref{subsec:bg-pqc-primitives}, is that quantum-resistant primitives
carry larger keys and signatures than the schemes they replace, which collides
directly with the memory and energy budgets of the constrained devices that the
fast layer is meant to serve~\cite{hanna2025comprehensive, cruz2025measuring}.

Across the three subsections, a consistent shape emerges. Learning-based
detection supplies an accurate but mutable trust verdict; ledger provenance makes
that verdict immutable but forces a choice between speed and public
verifiability; hybrid and tiered ledgers dissolve that choice but inherit a
bridge to secure and a quantum horizon to survive. No single reviewed system
carries all three concerns at once under the resource budget of constrained
industrial operation. Section~\ref{sec:review-gap} consolidates the specific gaps
this leaves and the questions the remainder of the work addresses.

\section{Summary of Review and Research Gaps}
\label{sec:review-gap}

The review assembled in this chapter set out to locate, against a reproducible
corpus rather than a convenient selection, where existing security architectures
fall short of a clear requirement: that trustworthy security for constrained
industrial operation must be at once behavioural, verifiable, and affordable,
and must remain so against an adversary whose cryptographic reach grows with
time. The state of the art advances each of these properties in isolation and,
read across its three strands, exposes a coherent set of four gaps that no
reviewed system closes together.

\textbf{Gap G1: the mutable trust verdict.} Learning-based detection has become
accurate, temporally aware, privacy-preserving through federation, and in the
best work calibrated and explainable. Yet the verdict it produces is computed and
stored wherever the model runs, and is therefore alterable by whoever controls
that host. Accuracy without immutability cannot underwrite an accountable
security decision, and the reviewed detectors do not, on their own, make their
output tamper-evident.

\textbf{Gap G2: the placement dilemma.} Ledger provenance supplies the
immutability that detection lacks, but the reviewed systems force a choice
between two incompatible virtues. A permissioned ledger fast enough for
high-frequency behavioural commitment is not public enough to be externally
verifiable, while a public ledger open enough for external verification is not
fast enough for operational commitment. No single-ledger design surveyed
reconciles operational speed with independent verifiability within a constrained
budget.

\textbf{Gap G3: durability and its cost.} Hybrid and tiered ledgers dissolve the
placement dilemma by composing a fast permissioned layer with a public anchor
joined through cross-chain anchoring, and this is the structural pattern on which
a partitioned architecture depends. The composition, however, inherits two
unresolved liabilities: the cross-chain link becomes the weakest point of the
combined system, and the tamper-evidence of the anchored record rests on
cryptography that a quantum adversary would defeat, so that post-quantum
migration becomes mandatory yet collides with the memory and energy budgets of
the very devices the fast layer serves.

\textbf{Gap G4: no composed, portable system.} A fourth gap cuts across the other
three. The reviewed corpus contains detectors, ledgers, and hybrid designs, but
no single system carries behavioural detection, immutable and externally
verifiable provenance, and quantum-resilient protection at once under the
resource envelope of constrained industrial hardware. Where composed systems do
appear, the organising principle behind them is demonstrated on a single
implementation, leaving open whether trust partitioning is a
substrate-independent principle or an artefact of one design. The evaluations
that would settle this, spanning detection performance, resource overhead,
blockchain-side cost, and quantum resilience together, are largely absent.

These gaps motivate the five research questions stated in
Section~\ref{sec:rq}, and Fig.~\ref{fig:gaps-rq} traces each question to the gaps
from which it follows. RQ1, on partitioning trust across a hybrid ledger within
device budgets, follows from G2 and G3 taken together. RQ2, on lightweight
behavioural trust scoring, follows from G1 and from the affordability
requirement. RQ3, on quantum-resilient identity and credential delivery, follows
from the durability half of G3. RQ4, on the substrate independence of trust
partitioning, follows from G4. RQ5, on isolating a misbehaving node under
adversarial conditions, follows from the accountability thread that runs through
all four gaps.

Answering these questions requires an architecture that reconciles scalability,
speed, and security while carrying out validation and anchoring within the
resource limits of industrial edge hardware. The design of that architecture,
and the trust-partitioning principle that organises it, is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{Chapter_4}.
